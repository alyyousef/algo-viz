**BLEU** (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) is an algorithm for evaluating the quality of text which has been machine-translated from one natural language to another. 

Before BLEU, the only way to evaluate translation quality was to hire human translators to manually score outputs, which was slow and expensive. BLEU revolutionized the field by providing a fast, language-independent, automated metric that correlates highly with human judgment.

## How BLEU Works

The core idea behind BLEU is simple: A machine translation is considered "good" if it closely matches a professional human translation.

BLEU calculates a score (ranging from 0.0 to 1.0) by comparing the machine-generated text (the **candidate**) to one or more human-generated texts (the **references**).

### 1. N-gram Precision
BLEU's foundation is **modified n-gram precision**. It checks how many *n-grams* (sequences of *n* words) in the candidate translation appear in the reference translations.

*   **Unigrams (1-gram):** Checking single words. This measures *adequacy* (did the machine get the vocabulary right?).
*   **Bigrams (2-gram), Trigrams (3-gram), etc.:** Checking sequences of 2 or 3 words. This measures *fluency* (is the grammar and word order correct?).

If a machine simply repeats the same correct word over and over ("the the the the"), standard precision would be 100%. BLEU uses *modified* precision, which caps the count of a word in the candidate by the maximum number of times that word appears in the reference.

### 2. The Brevity Penalty (BP)
N-gram precision has a major flaw: very short translations can score unfairly high. If the reference is "I am going to the store" and the machine just outputs "I am", the precision is 100%. 

To fix this, BLEU introduces a **Brevity Penalty**. If the machine translation is shorter than the reference translation, the final score is penalized.

### 3. The Final Formula
The final BLEU score is the geometric mean of the modified n-gram precisions (usually up to 4-grams, denoted as BLEU-4), multiplied by the Brevity Penalty.

<Callout icon="chart-line" title="Interpreting BLEU Scores">
BLEU scores are often multiplied by 100 to be read as a percentage.
*   **< 10:** Almost useless.
*   **10 - 20:** Hard to get the gist.
*   **20 - 30:** The gist is clear, but grammar is poor.
*   **30 - 40:** Understandable to good translations.
*   **40 - 50:** High quality translations.
*   **> 50:** Very high quality, adequate, and fluent translations. (Note: Even human translators rarely score above 60 when compared against each other, because language is subjective).
</Callout>

## Limitations of BLEU

While ubiquitous, BLEU is heavily criticized in modern NLP:
1.  **Meaningless on a Sentence Level:** BLEU was designed to be computed over an entire *corpus* (thousands of sentences). Calculating a BLEU score for a single sentence is mathematically unreliable and often yields 0.
2.  **No Understanding of Semantics:** BLEU only looks for exact string matches. If the reference is "The car is fast" and the machine outputs "The automobile is quick", the BLEU score is 0, even though the translation is perfect. It does not understand synonyms.
3.  **Language Dependency:** BLEU scores cannot be compared across different languages. A BLEU of 30 on English-to-Spanish is not equivalent to a BLEU of 30 on English-to-Chinese.

## Summary

BLEU is the granddaddy of NLP evaluation metrics. While newer metrics like **ROUGE** (for summarization), **METEOR** (which handles synonyms), and neural metrics like **BERTScore** are becoming more popular, BLEU remains a mandatory baseline for any machine translation task.
