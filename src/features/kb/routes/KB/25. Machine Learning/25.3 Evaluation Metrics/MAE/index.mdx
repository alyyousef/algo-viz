**Mean Absolute Error (MAE)** is one of the simplest and most commonly used evaluation metrics for regression models (models that predict a continuous numerical value, like price or temperature).

It measures the average magnitude of the errors in a set of predictions, without considering their direction (i.e., whether the prediction was too high or too low).

## The Formula

The formula for MAE is very straightforward:

$$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$$

Where:
*   $n$ is the total number of observations.
*   $y_i$ is the actual, true value.
*   $\hat{y}_i$ is the model's predicted value.
*   $|y_i - \hat{y}_i|$ is the absolute value of the error. (If the true value is 10 and you predict 12, the error is $|10 - 12| = |-2| = 2$).

## Key Characteristics of MAE

### 1. High Interpretability
The biggest advantage of MAE is that it is highly interpretable because it is on the **same scale** as the data being predicted. 

If you are building a model to predict the price of houses in dollars, and your MAE is 15,000, you can literally say: "On average, my model's predictions are off by $15,000." This is incredibly easy to explain to non-technical stakeholders.

### 2. Robustness to Outliers
Unlike MSE (Mean Squared Error), MAE treats all errors equally, regardless of their size. An error of 10 is penalized exactly twice as much as an error of 5.

Because it does not square the errors, MAE is **robust to outliers**. If your dataset has a few massive anomalies (e.g., one house that sold for $100 million when the rest are $500k), those anomalies will not disproportionately drag the MAE score up. 

<Callout icon="scale-balanced" title="When to use MAE vs MSE?">
*   **Use MAE** when your dataset contains outliers that are likely data entry errors or flukes that you want the model to largely ignore.
*   **Use MSE** when large errors are disproportionately worse than small errors and you want the model to be heavily penalized for making massive mistakes, even if they are rare.
</Callout>

## Disadvantages of MAE

The main mathematical disadvantage of MAE comes up during model training (specifically when using Gradient Descent). 

Because the MAE formula uses the absolute value function, its derivative is not continuous. It has a sharp "V" shape at zero, meaning the gradient is undefined exactly at zero. While there are workarounds (like subgradients), it makes optimizing for MAE slightly mathematically clumsier than optimizing for the smooth, convex bowl of MSE.
