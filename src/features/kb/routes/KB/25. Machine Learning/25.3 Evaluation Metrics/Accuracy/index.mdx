**Accuracy** is the most intuitive and commonly understood performance metric for classification models. It simply measures the proportion of total predictions that the model got correct.

## The Formula

The formula for accuracy is straightforward:

$$\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Number of Correct Predictions}}{\text{Total Number of Predictions}}$$

In the context of binary classification (using a Confusion Matrix), it is expressed as:

$$\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{True Positives (TP)} + \text{True Negatives (TN)}}{\text{TP} + \text{TN} + \text{False Positives (FP)} + \text{False Negatives (FN)}}$$

## When is Accuracy a Good Metric?

Accuracy is a perfectly fine metric **ONLY** when your dataset is perfectly **balanced** and the cost of false positives and false negatives are roughly the same.

For example, if you are classifying images of cats and dogs, and exactly 50% of your images are cats and 50% are dogs, an accuracy of 90% is a genuinely good indicator of the model's performance.

## The Problem: The Accuracy Paradox

Accuracy becomes highly misleading—even dangerous—when dealing with **imbalanced datasets**.

<Callout icon="exclamation-triangle" title="The Accuracy Paradox Example">
Imagine you are building a model to detect a rare disease that affects exactly 1 in 10,000 people (0.01% of the population).

You build a completely "dumb" model that simply predicts "Healthy" for every single person, regardless of their symptoms.

What is the accuracy of this dumb model?
**99.99%**

If you only look at accuracy, you might think you've built the greatest medical AI in history. In reality, your model is completely useless and will fail to identify the 1 person who actually has the disease.
</Callout>

This phenomenon is known as the Accuracy Paradox. The model optimizes for the majority class and completely ignores the minority class, yet still achieves an incredibly high score.

## What to use instead?

Whenever your classes are imbalanced (e.g., fraud detection, spam filtering, rare disease diagnosis, click-through rate prediction), you must use metrics that look deeper than raw accuracy:

*   **Precision:** Out of all the positive predictions we made, how many were actually positive?
*   **Recall (Sensitivity):** Out of all the actual positive cases, how many did we successfully find?
*   **F1 Score:** The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. A much better single-number metric for imbalanced data.
*   **ROC-AUC:** Measures the model's ability to rank positive instances higher than negative instances across all thresholds.

## Summary

Accuracy is a trap for beginners. While it is easy to understand, it should almost never be the sole metric used to evaluate a machine learning model unless you are absolutely certain your class distribution is balanced.
