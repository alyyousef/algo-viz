**Mean Squared Error (MSE)** is the most common loss function and evaluation metric used for regression models (models that predict continuous numerical values).

It measures the average of the squares of the errors—that is, the average squared difference between the estimated values and the actual value.

## The Formula

The formula for MSE is:

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

Where:
*   $n$ is the total number of observations.
*   $y_i$ is the actual, true value.
*   $\hat{y}_i$ is the model's predicted value.
*   $(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ is the squared error. 

## Key Characteristics of MSE

### 1. Heavily Penalizes Large Errors
Because the errors are squared *before* they are averaged, MSE heavily penalizes large errors. 

If the true value is 10:
*   Predicting 11 (error of 1) contributes $1^2 = 1$ to the MSE sum.
*   Predicting 15 (error of 5) contributes $5^2 = 25$ to the MSE sum.

An error that is 5 times larger is penalized **25 times more**. This means a model optimizing for MSE will prioritize avoiding massive, catastrophic errors at the cost of being slightly wrong on average.

<Callout icon="exclamation-triangle" title="Sensitivity to Outliers">
The flip side of this heavy penalty is that MSE is extremely sensitive to outliers. If your dataset contains a few massive anomalies (e.g., bad data entry), the model will warp its entire line of best fit just to try and reduce the massive squared error on those few outlier points, often ruining predictions for the rest of the normal data. If outliers are a problem, you should use **MAE (Mean Absolute Error)** instead.
</Callout>

### 2. Perfect for Gradient Descent
From a mathematical perspective, MSE is highly preferred as a loss function for training models (like Neural Networks and Linear Regression). 

The squaring function $x^2$ is smooth, continuous, and strictly convex (shaped like a perfect bowl). This means it has exactly one global minimum, and its derivative is very easy to calculate everywhere. This guarantees that optimization algorithms like Gradient Descent will smoothly and reliably find the optimal weights.

## RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)

One problem with MSE is that the final number is not in the same units as the original data. If you are predicting the price of a house in dollars, your MSE will be in *squared dollars*, which makes no intuitive sense.

To fix this, we often take the square root of the MSE, creating the **RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)**:

$$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\text{MSE}}$$

RMSE retains all the mathematical properties of MSE (it still heavily penalizes large errors), but it scales the final number back down to the original units, making it much easier to interpret and report to stakeholders.
