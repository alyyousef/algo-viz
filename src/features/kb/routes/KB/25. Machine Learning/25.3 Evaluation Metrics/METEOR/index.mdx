**METEOR** (Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit ORdering) is an evaluation metric for Natural Language Processing, primarily used to assess Machine Translation.

It was developed specifically to address the major flaws of the **BLEU** score. While BLEU is rigid and only looks for exact word matches, METEOR is flexible and understands that humans use synonyms and different word forms to express the exact same idea.

## The Flaw of BLEU

Suppose the human reference translation is: *"The quick automobile."*
If the machine translation outputs: *"The fast car."*

A human would give this a perfect score. They mean the exact same thing. 
However, BLEU only looks for exact string matches. Because "fast" != "quick" and "car" != "automobile", BLEU would give this translation a terrible score (0% beyond the word "The").

## How METEOR Fixes This

METEOR aligns the machine translation (hypothesis) with the human translation (reference) in stages, using increasingly flexible matching criteria:

1.  **Exact Match:** Just like BLEU, it first tries to match exact strings (e.g., "The" == "The").
2.  **Stem Match:** If words don't match exactly, it reduces words to their root stem using a stemmer (like the Snowball stemmer). It will successfully match "computer" with "computing" or "computers".
3.  **Synonym Match:** It uses external lexical databases (like **WordNet**) to check if the unmatched words are synonyms. This allows it to successfully match "automobile" with "car", or "fast" with "quick".

### Calculating the Score

Once the alignment is complete, METEOR calculates a score based on precision and recall. 

Crucially, **METEOR heavily favors Recall** over Precision. (In its default configuration, recall is weighted 9 times heavier than precision). The creators of METEOR found through empirical testing that a high recall correlates much more strongly with human judgment than a high precision.

### The Fragmentation Penalty

METEOR also includes a penalty for bad word order. If the machine outputs the correct words but they are scrambled completely out of order, the alignment will be "fragmented" into many small, disconnected chunks. 

The fewer contiguous chunks there are (meaning the word order closely matches the reference), the higher the final score.

<Callout icon="thumbs-up" title="Why isn't METEOR used everywhere?">
Despite correlating much better with human judgment than BLEU, METEOR is less universally used for two reasons:
1. It is mathematically much more complex and slower to calculate.
2. Because it relies on stemming and WordNet, it is **language-dependent**. It only works for languages that have a well-developed WordNet database (like English, French, Spanish, etc.), whereas BLEU can theoretically be run on any language.
</Callout>

## Summary

METEOR is a massive improvement over BLEU when you want an evaluation metric that actually attempts to capture the *meaning* of the translation rather than just checking if the strings are identical. It remains a highly respected metric in modern NLP evaluation suites.
