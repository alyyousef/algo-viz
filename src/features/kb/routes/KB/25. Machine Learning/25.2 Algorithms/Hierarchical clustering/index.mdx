**Hierarchical Clustering** is an unsupervised learning algorithm used to group similar objects into clusters. Unlike K-Means, which creates a single "flat" set of clusters, hierarchical clustering creates a tree-like nested structure of clusters called a **dendrogram**.

There are two main approaches to building this hierarchy: Agglomerative and Divisive.

## 1. Agglomerative (Bottom-Up)

This is the most common approach. 
1.  **Start:** Treat every single data point as its own individual cluster (if you have 100 points, you start with 100 clusters).
2.  **Merge:** Find the two clusters that are closest to each other and merge them into a new, larger cluster.
3.  **Repeat:** Repeat step 2 until all data points are merged into a single giant root cluster.

## 2. Divisive (Top-Down)

This is the exact opposite of Agglomerative clustering.
1.  **Start:** Treat all data points as belonging to one single giant cluster.
2.  **Split:** Find the most heterogeneous (dissimilar) cluster and split it into two.
3.  **Repeat:** Repeat step 2 until every single data point is in its own individual cluster.

<Callout icon="chart-network" title="The Dendrogram">
The result of hierarchical clustering is visualized using a dendrogram. The y-axis represents the distance (dissimilarity) between clusters. To get a specific number of flat clusters, you draw a horizontal line across the dendrogram at a specific y-value; the number of vertical lines you intersect is the number of clusters.
</Callout>

## Measuring Distance (Linkage Criteria)

A crucial part of the algorithm is determining how to measure the "distance" between two clusters that contain multiple points. This is called the **linkage criterion**:

*   **Single Linkage:** The distance between two clusters is the distance between their *closest* points. (Tends to create long, chaining clusters).
*   **Complete Linkage:** The distance between two clusters is the distance between their *furthest* points. (Tends to create compact, spherical clusters).
*   **Average Linkage:** The average of all distances between all pairs of points in the two clusters.
*   **Ward's Method:** Merges the two clusters that result in the smallest increase in total within-cluster variance (highly effective and commonly used).

## Advantages and Disadvantages

### Advantages
*   **No $k$ required:** You don't need to specify the number of clusters in advance. You can decide later by looking at the dendrogram.
*   **Rich Hierarchy:** The dendrogram provides deep insight into the structure and relationships within the data at various levels of granularity.

### Disadvantages
*   **Computationally Expensive:** The standard agglomerative algorithm has a time complexity of $O(n^3)$ (or $O(n^2 \log n)$ optimized), making it entirely unsuitable for very large datasets.
*   **Irreversible:** Once a merge or split is made, it cannot be undone in later steps. If the algorithm makes a poor decision early on, it cannot correct it.
