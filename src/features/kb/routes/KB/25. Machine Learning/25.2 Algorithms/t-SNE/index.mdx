**t-SNE** (t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) is a machine learning algorithm for dimensionality reduction developed by Laurens van der Maaten and Geoffrey Hinton. Unlike PCA, which is a linear technique focused on maximizing variance, t-SNE is a **non-linear** technique specifically designed for **visualization**.

Its primary goal is to take high-dimensional data (e.g., images with thousands of pixels, or word embeddings with hundreds of dimensions) and map it into a 2D or 3D space so that humans can see the natural clusters and underlying structure.

## How t-SNE Works

t-SNE works in two main stages, both relying on converting distances into probabilities.

### 1. High-Dimensional Space
First, it calculates the similarity between pairs of points in the high-dimensional space. It uses a Gaussian (Normal) distribution. If two points are very close together, they have a high probability of being "neighbors". If they are far apart, the probability is near zero.

### 2. Low-Dimensional Space
Next, it maps the points into a low-dimensional space (2D or 3D) randomly. It then calculates the similarities between points in this new space. 
Crucially, in the low-dimensional space, t-SNE uses a **Student's t-distribution** (specifically with 1 degree of freedom, which looks like a Bell curve but with much "fatter" tails) instead of a Gaussian distribution.

### 3. The Optimization (Minimizing KL Divergence)
t-SNE then uses an optimization algorithm (Gradient Descent) to move the points around in the 2D space until the probabilities in the 2D space match the probabilities in the high-dimensional space as closely as possible. 
The mathematical metric it minimizes to achieve this match is called the **Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence**.

<Callout icon="lightbulb" title="The 'Crowding Problem'">
Why use a t-distribution in the low-dimensional space? Because there is much less "room" in 2D than in 100D. If you tried to map 100D points into 2D using the same Gaussian distribution, points that were moderately far apart would be crushed together in the center of the 2D map. The "fat tails" of the t-distribution allow t-SNE to place moderately distant points much further apart in 2D, preserving the local cluster structures perfectly.
</Callout>

## Important Caveats of t-SNE

While t-SNE produces beautiful visualizations, it is notoriously easily misinterpreted if you don't understand its limitations:

1.  **Hyperparameter Sensitivity (Perplexity):** The `perplexity` parameter roughly controls how many nearest neighbors each point considers. Changing this value can radically alter the shape of the visualization. You should always run t-SNE with multiple perplexity values (usually between 5 and 50).
2.  **Distance is Meaningless:** t-SNE only preserves *local* neighborhood structure, not global structure. The distance between two distinct clusters in a t-SNE plot means almost nothing. Just because Cluster A is near Cluster B on the plot does *not* mean they are similar in the high-dimensional space.
3.  **Cluster Size is Meaningless:** A dense, tight cluster in t-SNE might actually be spread out in reality. t-SNE expands dense regions and compresses sparse regions to make the visualization look nice.
4.  **No New Data:** t-SNE cannot be applied to new, unseen data. It is an embedding algorithm, not a predictive model. If you get new data, you have to rerun the entire algorithm from scratch.

## Summary

t-SNE is an unparalleled tool for data exploration and visualizing the local structure of complex datasets. However, it is strictly an exploratory tool; its outputs should never be used as inputs for downstream clustering (like K-Means) or classification models due to its distortion of global distances.
