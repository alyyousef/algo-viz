**K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)** is one of the simplest supervised machine learning algorithms. It is a **non-parametric**, **lazy learning** algorithm used for both classification and regression.

## The Core Concept

The premise of KNN is incredibly intuitive: "Birds of a feather flock together." It assumes that similar things exist in close proximity.

To make a prediction for a new, unseen data point, the algorithm:
1.  Calculates the distance between the new point and every single point in the training dataset.
2.  Selects the $K$ points in the training data that are closest to the new point (the "nearest neighbors").
3.  **For Classification:** The new point is assigned the class that is most common among its $K$ nearest neighbors (a majority vote).
4.  **For Regression:** The new point is assigned the average (mean) value of the target variables of its $K$ nearest neighbors.

## What is "Lazy Learning"?

KNN is called a "lazy" learner because it does not have a training phase in the traditional sense. 
*   An "eager" learner (like a Neural Network or Logistic Regression) builds a generalized model during training and discards the training data.
*   A "lazy" learner like KNN does zero work during "training"—it simply stores the entire dataset in memory. All the computational heavy lifting is deferred until a prediction is requested.

<Callout icon="zap" title="Performance Implications">
Because of its lazy nature, KNN has **$O(1)$ training time**, but **$O(nd)$ inference time** (where $n$ is the number of samples and $d$ is the number of dimensions). Making predictions on a large dataset can be incredibly slow, though data structures like KD-Trees and Ball Trees can speed this up.
</Callout>

## Hyperparameters and Tuning

KNN only has two primary hyperparameters to tune:

### 1. The Value of $K$
$K$ dictates how many neighbors influence the prediction.
*   **Small $K$ (e.g., $K=1$):** High variance, low bias. The model perfectly captures the training data (a $K=1$ model always has 0% error on the training set) but is highly sensitive to noise and outliers, leading to **overfitting**.
*   **Large $K$:** Low variance, high bias. The model becomes overly generalized, smoothing out the decision boundaries, potentially leading to **underfitting**.

### 2. The Distance Metric
How do we measure "closeness"?
*   **Euclidean Distance:** The straight-line distance (most common for continuous data).
*   **Manhattan Distance:** The sum of absolute differences across all dimensions.
*   **Minkowski Distance:** A generalization of both Euclidean and Manhattan.
*   **Cosine Similarity:** Often used for text data (TF-IDF vectors).

<Callout icon="exclamation-triangle" title="Feature Scaling is Mandatory">
Because KNN relies entirely on distance calculations, features on larger scales will dominate the distance metric. For example, a feature ranging from 1 to 1000 will overpower a feature ranging from 0 to 1. **You must normalize or standardize your data before using KNN.**
</Callout>

## The Curse of Dimensionality

KNN suffers severely from the "Curse of Dimensionality." As the number of features (dimensions) increases, the volume of the space increases exponentially, making the available data sparse. In very high-dimensional space, all data points become roughly equidistant from each other, rendering the concept of "nearest neighbors" meaningless.
