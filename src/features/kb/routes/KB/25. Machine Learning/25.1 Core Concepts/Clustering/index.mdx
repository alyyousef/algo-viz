**Clustering** is a core technique in Unsupervised Learning. Unlike classification (where you tell the algorithm "these are pictures of cats and dogs, learn the difference"), in clustering, you hand the algorithm a massive pile of unlabeled data and say, "group the similar ones together."

It is primarily used for **Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)**, customer segmentation, anomaly detection, and discovering hidden structures in data.

## 1. K-Means Clustering

The most famous and widely used clustering algorithm.

**How it works:**
1. You must explicitly tell the algorithm how many clusters you want (the 'K' value). Let's say $K=3$.
2. The algorithm randomly drops 3 "Centroids" (points) into the dataset.
3. Every data point is assigned to its nearest Centroid.
4. The Centroid moves to the actual center (mean) of all the points assigned to it.
5. Repeat steps 3 and 4 until the Centroids stop moving.

**Pros:** Extremely fast and scalable.
**Cons:** You have to guess 'K'. It struggles heavily with non-circular clusters or clusters of varying sizes. It is also sensitive to outliers.

## 2. DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise)

Instead of looking for mathematical centers, DBSCAN looks for areas of high density separated by areas of low density.

**How it works:**
1. You define two parameters: $epsilon$ (a radius distance) and $min\_samples$ (the minimum points required inside that radius to be considered a dense region).
2. It groups together points that are closely packed together, expanding the cluster as long as density remains high.
3. Points that are in low-density regions are marked as Noise (Outliers).

**Pros:** You don't have to specify $K$. It can find clusters of completely arbitrary shapes (e.g., a cluster shaped like a crescent moon). It automatically isolates outliers.
**Cons:** Struggles if the clusters have vastly different densities.

## 3. Hierarchical Clustering

Instead of assigning points to flat, separate groups, it builds a tree of clusters (a **Dendrogram**).

**How it works (Agglomerative):**
1. Start by treating every single data point as its own cluster.
2. Find the two clusters that are closest together and merge them.
3. Repeat step 2 until all points are merged into one giant single cluster.
4. The human data scientist looks at the resulting tree diagram and "cuts" it at the desired depth to get the final clusters.

**Pros:** No need to guess $K$. Provides a beautiful visual hierarchy.
**Cons:** Very computationally expensive. Unusable on massive datasets.

<Callout icon="lightbulb" title="How do you measure similarity?">
All clustering algorithms rely on a mathematical definition of "distance" between two data points. The most common is **Euclidean Distance** (straight line distance). However, if your data has many dimensions (e.g., 100 columns), Euclidean distance breaks down due to the "Curse of Dimensionality". In high dimensions, you might use **Cosine Similarity** instead.
</Callout>
