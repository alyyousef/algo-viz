**Swish** is a modern activation function discovered in 2017 by researchers at Google Brain. 

Fascinatingly, Swish was not designed by a human mathematician. It was discovered using an automated Neural Architecture Search (NAS). The researchers tasked an AI with generating and testing thousands of random mathematical equations to find the best possible activation function. The AI returned Swish, which consistently outperforms standard ReLU on massive, deep neural networks.

## The Formula

**Formula:** $f(x) = x \cdot \text{sigmoid}(\beta x)$

*(Where $\beta$ is either a fixed constant or a learnable parameter. If $\beta = 1$, it is standard Swish, sometimes called SiLU).*

Swish simply takes the input $x$ and multiplies it by the Sigmoid of $x$. 

## Why is it better than ReLU?

For years, **ReLU** ($f(x) = \max(0, x)$) was the absolute standard. However, ReLU has a major flaw: the "Dying ReLU" problem. If an input is less than zero, ReLU violently crushes it to exactly $0.00$. This creates a sharp, non-differentiable corner. If a neuron's weights are pushed into negative territory, it outputs zero, its gradient becomes zero, and the neuron "dies," never updating again for the rest of training.

Swish solves this by being **smooth and non-monotonic**.

If you graph Swish:
*   For large positive numbers, it acts exactly like ReLU (a straight line going up).
*   **The Magic:** As numbers drop slightly below zero, Swish does *not* flatten to zero immediately. Instead, it smoothly curves down into negative territory (creating a small "bump" below the axis) before slowly curving back up toward zero for massive negative numbers.

### The Benefits of the "Bump"
That smooth, negative bump is the secret to Swish's success. 
1.  **Smoothness:** Because it is perfectly smooth everywhere, the optimizer has a much easier time calculating gradients, preventing the chaos caused by ReLU's sharp corner.
2.  **Negative Gradients:** Because small negative numbers are allowed to pass through (and generate a gradient), neurons that are pushed slightly negative don't instantly "die." They can still be mathematically rescued by the optimizer and pushed back into the positive range later in training.

<Callout icon="check-circle" title="Where is it used?">
Swish is slightly more computationally expensive to calculate than ReLU (because it requires calculating $e^{-x}$). However, the performance gains are so significant that it is the default activation function for Google's famous **EfficientNet** architecture, and it is heavily utilized in many modern large language models.
</Callout>
