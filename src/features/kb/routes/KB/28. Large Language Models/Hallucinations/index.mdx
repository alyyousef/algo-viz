An AI **Hallucination** occurs when a Large Language Model (LLM) generates a response that is grammatically perfect, sounds highly confident, but is completely factually incorrect or entirely fabricated.

For example, if you ask an LLM: *"Who was the first person to walk on the sun?"*
An older, unaligned LLM might reply: *"The first person to walk on the sun was astronaut James Carmichael in 1978 during NASA's Apollo 18 mission."*

This is the single biggest barrier to enterprise adoption of Generative AI. In a legal context, LLMs have notoriously hallucinated fake legal precedents, causing lawyers to submit fake cases to a judge.

## Why do LLMs Hallucinate?

To understand hallucinations, you must understand a terrifying truth: **LLMs do not know what a "fact" is.**

An LLM is not querying a database of truth. It is a statistical engine. Its only goal is to predict the next statistically likely word based on the patterns it learned from the internet. 
When asked about walking on the sun, the model doesn't "know" it's impossible. It simply recognizes the pattern: `["first person to walk on"]` is usually followed by `["astronaut"]` and `["NASA"]` and a year. So it generates a mathematically probable (but factually absurd) sentence.

### Causes of Hallucination:
1.  **Training Data Gaps:** If the model wasn't trained on a specific niche topic, it will guess the answer rather than admit it doesn't know.
2.  **Context Window Overload:** If you paste a 50-page document into the prompt, the model can suffer from "Attention dilution." It might accidentally pull a name from Page 1 and a date from Page 50 and mash them together into a false statement.
3.  **Sycophancy:** LLMs are trained to be "helpful." If a user asks a highly leading, incorrect question (*"Tell me about the time Abraham Lincoln invented the iPhone"*), the model will often hallucinate a story just to please the user, rather than correcting them.

## How do we fix it?

While hallucinations cannot be mathematically reduced to 0%, the AI industry has developed several robust methods to mitigate them:

1.  **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation):** The absolute gold standard for fixing hallucinations. Instead of relying on the LLM's internal memory, you force the LLM to search a real database, retrieve a factual document, and write its answer *strictly* based on that document.
2.  **Grounding (System Prompts):** Explicitly instructing the model: *"If you do not know the answer with 100% certainty, you must reply 'I do not know.' Do not guess."*
3.  **Chain-of-Thought Prompting:** Forcing the LLM to write out its step-by-step logic. Often, by writing out the steps, the model catches its own logical errors before it outputs the final hallucinated answer.

<Callout icon="triangle-exclamation" title="Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Hallucinations">
*   **Intrinsic:** The LLM contradicts the specific information you gave it in the prompt. (You pasted a receipt for $5, and the LLM says you spent $10).
*   **Extrinsic:** The LLM hallucinates extra details that weren't in your prompt, but *might* be true based on its training data. (You ask for a summary of a movie, and it accurately names the director, even though you didn't provide it).
</Callout>
